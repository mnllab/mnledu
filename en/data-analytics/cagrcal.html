Why CAGR is not the average you first think of

If revenue went from $100M to $200M over three years, that is 100% total growth. Dividing by three to get 33% a year is wrong: growing 33% annually from $100M gives $236M after three years, not $200M.

Compound annual growth rate works backwards from the two endpoints and asks what steady annual rate would produce that result. For the example above it is roughly 26%. This is the figure used in market sizing and revenue projections, because it compounds the way money actually does.

When to use it

Market sizing in a business plan
Writing "the market grows at X% annually to $Y by 2030". Two data points from a source report are enough to derive the rate between them.
Revenue projections
Three to five year forecasts for a grant application or an investor deck. Set a defensible rate and get the year by year table.
Checking someone else's claim
Testing whether a growth rate quoted in a pitch deck matches the underlying numbers.
Comparing unlike businesses
Putting two operations of different sizes on the same footing by comparing rates rather than absolute figures.

Frequently asked questions

Does it handle a decline
Yes. A fall produces a negative rate, which is the correct way to express shrinkage on an annual basis.
How is the period counted
From 2020 to 2025 is five years. It is the interval between the two points, not the number of years listed.
Can I put this straight into a business plan
Use it as a starting point. Past growth does not guarantee future growth, and the first question a reviewer asks is why the rate should hold. Have the reasoning ready alongside the table.
