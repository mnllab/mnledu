Voice and rhythm are hard to practise separately

Singing is not only about finding the right note or counting the beat. You need to hold a pitch accurately, move naturally into the next beat, and line up your breath and entrance once accompaniment is involved.

That is why this tool shows where your voice is right now and whether your stroke is ahead of or behind the beat in one place. Feedback at the moment the sound happens is faster than trying to reconstruct it from memory afterwards.

Read the note name and octave together

The centre display shows the current note, frequency, and cents offset. The same note name can be one or two octaves higher or lower, so the octave is shown separately using the Korean vocal convention. C5 is displayed as the third octave.

Your lowest and highest notes are also recorded during the session. Rather than forcing your range wider, hold the notes you can produce comfortably for a moment so the record is more useful.

Build your rhythm instead of choosing a fixed answer

Every song needs a different pattern. Repeating only a basic strong–weak groove makes it difficult to practise triplets, broken patterns, or rests.

Choose 4, 8, 12, or 16 beats, then click each beat cell to cycle through strong, weak, and rest. The strong–weak, strong–weak–weak, and strong–weak–weak–weak patterns are only quick starting points; change them freely afterwards.

Ways to enter a stroke

⌨️ Keyboard input
Assign the same key to strong and weak for a single undifferentiated stroke. Assign different keys to compare the accent you entered as well. Each beat immediately reports PERFECT, GOOD, EARLY, LATE, or MISSED.
📱 Mobile input
Tap the Strong Input and Weak Input buttons below the pattern. You can practise with the same judgement rules even without a keyboard.
🙈 Blind mode
The pattern plays four times, then stays silent for two repeats. Keep entering strokes through the silent section to see accuracy and average timing error when the sound returns.

Two ways to set BPM

For a tempo you already know, type the number directly into the BPM field. The value is not forcibly changed while you are typing; it is checked only after you finish entering it.

If you do not know the exact BPM of a backing track, use tap averaging. Use the BPM Tap Meter or Set BPM by Tapping SPACE in the recording tab, then tap at least three times in time with the beat to calculate an average and apply it to the BPM field.

Record vocals and the metronome together

The recording tab saves microphone vocals and the app’s metronome click as one WebM audio file. Headphones are recommended so the click does not feed back into the microphone.

After recording, load a backing-track file and select Play Backing Track + Tap SPACE. Tap with the music: the first tap becomes the starting reference, and the average gap between later taps becomes the BPM. Preview the recording and backing track together, then use fine adjustment only if it is still needed.

Frequently asked questions

My microphone will not start
Allow microphone access in your browser. Permission prompts are generally more reliable on HTTPS or localhost than when opening a file by double-clicking it.
The octave looks different from what I expect
This tool follows the Korean vocal convention: C3 is first octave, C4 is second octave, and C5 is third octave. Background noise or strong harmonics can disturb detection, so try holding a single note steadily.
My tap BPM jumps around
An average of three or more taps is much steadier than the first two. If you miss a beat, pause for a moment and start again; previous taps reset automatically.
Where are recordings stored?
Recording is processed in this browser and then downloaded by you as a WebM file. It is not kept permanently by the browser, so download any take you want to keep.

Microphone input, pitch analysis, metronome playback, recording, and backing-track sync preview are all processed inside your browser. Audio is not sent to a separate server. Browser policies may require HTTPS or localhost for microphone and recording features.
